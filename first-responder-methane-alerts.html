The Fire Chief Should Know When the Inspector Knows

A one-page proposal: send landfill methane readings above the Lower Explosive Limit to the local fire department automatically

Proposal for legislators, EGLE, and fire chiefs. Prepared November 12, 2026. DRAFT.

by Trisha Kunst, a resident living near the Arbor Hills landfill

The problem

At Arbor Hills, a boundary gas probe east of Napier Road read 82.9 percent methane (1,658% of the Lower Explosive Limit) on June 12, 2026. EGLE received that reading on July 30, 2026, as one page inside the operator's 949-page quarterly Consent Judgment report (MiEnviro document 7045536706099143566, page 464). Readings above the Lower Explosive Limit at that probe go back to February 2020. The facts and sources are on the Monitor's methane migration page. Additional details are in Explosive Gas Beside Arbor Hills, and No One Told the Neighbors.

Michigan's rule tells the operator to "notify the director." It names no one else. Rule 433(4)(a) (Mich Admin Code R 299.4433) requires the operator, when methane above the Lower Explosive Limit is detected at the boundary, to "[i]mmediately take all necessary steps to ensure protection of human health and notify the director." The fire department, the utility digging next door, and the neighbors are not on the list. The first Arbor Hills-area fire department to see the readings got them from residents, in November 2026.

The model already exists

Hospitals do not wait for a patient's records to be mailed. Health information exchanges send an alert to the care team the moment a patient is admitted anywhere in the network. Environmental readings are simpler: they are already public records, with no patient consent to manage. The same pattern can carry a methane reading from the operator to the fire chief in minutes instead of weeks.

The proposal

  1. Change who gets told. Amend Rule 433(4) so that notice of methane above the Lower Explosive Limit at or beyond the facility boundary goes to the local fire department at the same time as the director, and to utilities and property owners within a set distance (EGLE's 2025 methane guidance uses 1,500 feet from waste as its starting point for investigation).
  2. File readings as data, within days. Require boundary-probe readings to be entered in MiEnviro as structured data (probe, date, percent methane, percent of the Lower Explosive Limit) within a few days of each reading, instead of as pages in a quarterly report.
  3. Route by location. Every probe has a location. Match it to fire-district and utility service areas, so each alert reaches whoever covers that ground, along with the EGLE district inspector.
  4. Deliver in the standard emergency-alert format. Send each alert as a Common Alerting Protocol (CAP) message, the digital standard that links computer-aided dispatch (CAD) systems with public warning systems, and by email or text to a registered contact. Give registered responders a sign-in to see the full reading history for their district.

Why this is achievable now

  • MiEnviro already accepts structured forms from regulated facilities.
  • Fire-district boundaries and probe locations are both mapped.
  • Alerting by location is routine technology in health care, weather, and emergency management.
  • The rule change is one sentence: add the local fire department to the notice requirement.

What to ask for

  • Legislators and EGLE: add the local fire department, nearby utilities, and nearby owners to Rule 433(4) notice, and require boundary-probe readings to be filed as data within days.
  • Fire chiefs: ask EGLE's district office to tell you directly whenever a landfill in your district reports methane above the Lower Explosive Limit at its boundary.
