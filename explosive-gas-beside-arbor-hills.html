Explosive Gas Beside Arbor Hills, and No One Told the Neighbors

Nov. 15, 2026, from public records

The proposal that follows from this: The Fire Chief Should Know When the Inspector Knows →

On June 25, 2026, an ordinary summer morning in Michigan, probe AHMP15R2 read 68 percent methane with 0 percent oxygen: the normal air in the soil there had been pushed out completely. The probe sits just north of some railroad tracks and a tributary to Johnson Creek east of Napier Road. Driving north on Napier from Five Mile Road, the road crosses the railroad tracks and then a small bridge over the creek. The probe is about 800 feet farther north, on private land just east of the road.

The good news first: methane this rich will not explode where it sits. Methane needs to be 5-15% of the gas and mix with oxygen present to explode. That is why 5% methane is called the Lower Explosive Limit in regulations. Two dangers lie with 68% methane:

  1. If this gas leaks into an enclosed space (like a basement) and mixes with air, it passes through the 5 to 15 percent explosive range, so an explosive mixture can form without warning.
  2. Gas this rich can also push the oxygen out of a confined space such as a manhole or vault. That matters because people cannot sense low oxygen: a utility worker who climbs into a vault filled with this gas can pass out within moments, before noticing anything is wrong. Gas in a confined space can kill within minutes. In 2025, three men died in a Texas sewer manhole, each going in to rescue the one before.

These two dangers are taken seriously by regulations. Landfill operators are required to take action when methane is above the Lower Explosive Limit. Specifically:

  • Rule 433(4)(a): when methane tops the limit, the owner shall "immediately take all necessary steps to ensure protection of human health and notify the director."
  • Rule 433(4)(c): within 60 days, put a remediation plan in place and notify the director that it has been implemented.

The Arbor Hills probes are placed in a grid design, with the first row of probes just east of Napier, another row about 950 feet east of Napier, and the final row about 1,900 feet east of Napier. The final row of probes is the closest to the homes in the neighborhood. Those probe numbers are TGP19, TGP20, TGP21 and TGP22. None of these have ever read above the lower explosive limit, and all of them read 0% methane at their latest reading. TGP19 and TGP20 were last read Sep. 16, and TGP21 and TGP22 on June 17.

Let's jump into the story.

March 14, 2019: The state's Air Quality Division (then part of MDEQ, now EGLE) issues a 20-page Violation Notice to Advanced Disposal. "The Company has failed to implement a proper landfill gas collection system design to mitigate the subsurface migration of methane. Subsurface methane concentrations at perimeter monitoring probes have been exceeding regulatory limits since 2003."[1]

Nov. 20, 2019: EGLE's solid-waste program issues a "Violation Notice/Letter of Warning" from its Jackson District office. It falls between the Nov. 7 and Nov. 25, 2019 inspections, which both cited Rule 433 (gas monitoring and migration), 424 and 423. Marked high priority with a corrective action component in EGLE's waste database. (Advanced Disposal's Jan. 27, 2020 response is not public yet: it requires a paid FOIA request.)

April 2020: Isotope test #1, under the prior owner, Advanced Disposal: EIL compares methane from probe GMW-15R2 with landfill gas well EW22R. EIL's Nov. 5, 2020 report: "The results of the analyses supported concluding methane at GMW15R2 originated from the landfill." The lab results themselves have not been released publicly.

October 30, 2020: GFL takes ownership of Arbor Hills Landfill, Inc.

November 5, 2020: Six days after GFL takes over, the landfill's consultant EIL files a Supplemental Methane Monitoring Probe Installation Report with EGLE: new probes TGP17D/S and TGP18D/S about 950 feet east of Napier Road, and TGP19 through TGP22 along the east property line, about 1,900 feet east of Napier Road.

March 15-18, 2021: GFL's consultant drills 11 borings just west of Napier Road and finds up to 65 percent methane in the soil.

June 11, 2021: GFL sends EGLE a plan to install eight methane collector wells along the west side of Napier Road, starting in July 2021, "to address stray methane at GMW15R2."

March 7, 2022: The Honorable Wanda M. Stokes, at the 30th Judicial Circuit in Ingham County, enters Consent Judgment No. 2020-0593-CE, which requires Arbor Hills Landfill, Inc. (owned by GFL since October 2020) to install the gas extraction wells from its June 11, 2021 plan, comply with Rule 433, read five boundary probes weekly until each goes a year without an exceedance, and report every probe reading to EGLE each quarter (Paragraph 5.12). It also fines the company $355,109, settling claims that include the Rule 433(1)(b) boundary-methane violation. The company admitted no liability.

That same day, EGLE closes the 2019 Rule 433 violations in its database. The record does not say whether they were satisfied or simply absorbed into the Consent Judgment.

July 29, 2022: The first Consent Judgment quarterly report arrives at EGLE, including the probe methane readings. (The second-quarter 2022 report is the first one the Consent Judgment required.)

March 2022 through March 2023: No EGLE action on the underground gas found in the record for about a year, from the Consent Judgment to EGLE's March 22, 2023 statewide call. Meanwhile 15R2 reached 75.0% methane (May 25, 2022), and Q4 2022 had 15 readings over the limit.

March 3, 2023: Date of GFL's "Workplan to Mitigate LEL Exceedances," as listed in its consultant's 2024 report.[2] The plan has not been released publicly.

Do EGLE and the Attorney General track non-compliance the same way?

"You express concern that Arbor Hills' noncompliance is not documented because EGLE has not issued violations notices or stipulated penalties. EGLE uses the monitoring results that record methane exceedances to track and document Arbor Hills' noncompliance with the consent judgment's requirements."

Assistant Attorney General, ENRA Division, written reply of Nov. 6, 2026

In other words, EGLE tracks this noncompliance through the monitoring data, not through violation notices or stipulated penalties. Yet EGLE closed the 2019 Rule 433 violations in its database on March 7, 2022. The Consent Judgment names EGLE's Materials Management Division, which enforces the probe limits, to receive every quarterly probe report, starting in July 2022. No Materials Management action on the Arbor Hills exceedances has been found until a June 5, 2023 data request. This may be a good line of questioning for legislators.

Fast forward a year to 2023.

The first EGLE step regarding the methane exceedances came through EGLE's statewide review of gas migration plans: a call for every landfill's plan (March 22), then a request for five years of probe data (June 5).

March 22, 2023: Ahead of new state landfill gas rules that take effect March 29, 2023, EGLE puts out a call for all Michigan landfills to submit a Landfill Gas Migration Monitoring Plan.  Arbor Hills submits its plan on March 29, 2023.  (GFL's submittal; EGLE's March 22 letter itself has not been released)

June 5, 2023: Likely triggered by the EGLE statewide review on methane migration, EGLE requests five years of probe readings from Arbor Hills Landfill which EGLE does not appear to hold. Most of the probe readings it appears to hold are the ones reported quarterly under the court order since mid-2022. A week later, 15R2 reads 77.6% methane (June 13, 2023).

August 8, 2023: GFL sends EGLE the five years of probe readings (January 2019 to July 2023). Its map of "nearby structures" is a 2016 aerial photo, taken years before DTE began building its substation in 2024.

Sep. 21, 2023: Isotope test #2, at probe TGP18D. GFL's consultant later reports the results were not conclusive: some, such as tritium, "suggested a landfill source for at least a portion of the detected gas," while others did not, suggesting a mix of landfill and non-landfill gas.

December 20, 2023: Permit WRP039922 issued to DTE to "Install new electrical conduit along Napier Road and Five Mile Road in Northville Township."[3] The permit expires Dec. 20, 2028.

The conduit and stream crossings run within about 720 feet of probe 15R2, which has read up to 83% methane, and the trenches and duct bank can become a preferential pathway for gas. On Nov. 10, 2026, EGLE's water division confirmed in writing: "The probe readings were not part of the review for issuing permit WRP039922."

June 4, 2024: The only chemical lab test on record of the gas at probe 18D, taken by GFL's consultant SCS Engineers during its 2024 investigation (report, Table 3). The gas was 77.6% methane. The lab also found chloroform, chlorodifluoromethane and dichlorotetrafluoroethane (two refrigerant gases), heptane, toluene and dimethyl disulfide. Two of them were above health screening levels in the probe's gas, before any mixing with outdoor air:

  • Chloroform, which EPA screens as a cancer risk: 59.3 ppb, about 290 micrograms per cubic meter. That is roughly 70 times EPA's vapor-intrusion screening level for soil gas under a home (4.0 micrograms per cubic meter).[4]
  • Dimethyl disulfide, a sulfur compound with a strong garlic-like odor: 0.72 ppm, above the 0.5 ppm workplace limit (ACGIH, 8-hour shift).[5]

Benzene, vinyl chloride and hydrogen sulfide were tested for but not detected at 18D. The lab's detection limits were low enough to rule out benzene above EPA's soil-gas screening level, but not vinyl chloride or hydrogen sulfide.[4] No test of 15R2, 17D or the gas now coming out of the vents installed in September 2026 has been made public.

Aug. 28, 2024: EGLE issues DTE NPDES permit MI0060368 for construction dewatering on the same project, within about 720 feet of probe 15R2. Digging and dewatering run through 2025 and 2026. EGLE's Industrial Permits Unit has not yet answered whether the probe readings were considered.

October 9, 2024: After a March 2024 work plan and soil borings that summer, SCS Engineers sends EGLE its 2024 Gas Migration Investigation Report: "the AHE landfill is likely at least a partial source of the methane at temporary gas probe TGP18D."

February 2025: EGLE approves SCS's February 14, 2025 work plan to improve the existing perimeter gas wells.

April 10, 2025: EGLE approves guidance on development on or near methane generating sources.

The problem is never fixed.

August 27, 2026: Another probe goes over the limit for the first time: 17D, at 7.6% methane. 17D is the over-limit probe closest to homes, about 1,050 to 1,100 feet from the nearest houses. A month later, on Sep. 29, 2026, it reads 24.9% methane, about five times the explosive limit and its highest reading so far.

Sep. 28-29, 2026: GFL installs vents at the two spots methane is highest. Both are on the Wayne County side of Napier. No record shows that EGLE or GFL told the local fire department. A resident working with Arbor Hills Monitor contacted the fire chief by Nov. 6.

The released records show no tests of the gas other than the usual four-gas monitoring: methane, oxygen, carbon dioxide and balance gas. Nothing in the released records shows that non-methane organic compounds (NMOCs) present in the gas were considered before venting took place. Unlike the gas the landfill collects, which normally goes through sulfur treatment before it is burned in a flare or the power plant, this gas goes into the air untreated and unburned.

Remember: Methane at these levels is dangerous: it can explode once it mixes with air, and it can push out the oxygen people need to breathe. when methane tops the Lower Explosive Limit, Michigan's rule requires the landfill owner to "immediately take all necessary steps to ensure protection of human health and notify the director."

Sep. 29, 2026: the day after the first vent was installed: 15R2 reads 68.9% methane, down slightly from 73.8% on Sep. 25; 18D jumps from 0% to 19.3%. 17D had been declining, from 7.6% (Aug. 27) to 5.1% (Sep. 2) to 0% (Sep. 25); after the first vent went in on Sep. 28, it jumps to 24.9%. It is possible the process of installing the vents opened a pathway for that gas, or more gas, to reach 17D.

Oct. 2, 2026: An applicant asks EGLE for a permit (application HQR-Z2PX-RN8RE, EGLE file WRP041048) to fill 0.52 acres of wetland for "Coldwater West," a new housing development on vacant land along Ridge Road in Northville Township. The application lists Arbor Hills Landfill, Inc. as an adjacent property owner. Yet none of the 12 documents in the file, including EGLE's public notice, mentions methane, landfill gas or EGLE's guidance for development on or near methane generating sources. Public comment runs Nov. 4 to 24, 2026. On Nov. 12, a comment from a resident working with Arbor Hills Monitor asks whether the homes would be far enough from the methane.

Nov. 5, 2026: Arbor Hills Monitor publishes a first responder orientation map of the probes.

Nov. 6, 2026: After earlier calls, a resident working with Arbor Hills Monitor emails the local fire chief a link to the methane page, the probe layout and the responder map. Arbor Hills Monitor adds a first responder download: the probe readings and the map.

Nov. 12, 2026: Local fire chief, deputy chief and hazardous materials team are reviewing the probe data.

Nov. 13, 2026: EGLE's FOIA answer says GFL's Rule 433(4) notices and related correspondence for 2020 to 2024 do not exist as described.[6] For 2025 and 2026, EGLE released only the consultant's monthly and quarterly status reports and a February 2025 workplan, which are filed weeks or months after the readings, not "immediately" as the rule requires. Rule 433 names only EGLE's director; it requires no notice to local fire departments.

Nov. 13, 2026: More data arrives via FOIA E700318. It is discovered that the vents were installed at the end of September, and very little data is shared after that date. The next probe data is not due until the end of January 2027 (the Consent Judgment report by January 30; the consultant's quarterly update by the last business day of January), so no further data is expected until then, unless EGLE requests it from GFL.

What is known about the vents:

Not much. The day after the 15R2 vent was installed, 15R2 measured 68.9% methane (73.8% on Sep. 25), and 17D spiked from 0% on Sep. 25 to 24.9% methane. Then it was the end of the quarter. Data therefore has stopped flowing until January 2027.

What happens after March 2027

March 7, 2027 is the earliest date the methane probe requirements in Paragraph 5.12 of the Consent Judgment can end. The Consent Judgment says they terminate once GFL asks in writing, and EGLE files a Satisfaction of Judgment only after "full compliance."[7] EGLE's position is firmer. In a Nov. 6, 2026 written reply to Arbor Hills Monitor, the Assistant Attorney General wrote, "EGLE will not agree to terminate the requirements in Paragraph 5.12 until compliance is achieved." Neither document says how compliance will be measured.

If those requirements end, so do the probe reports GFL files under the Consent Judgment. GFL's duties under Rule 433 continue, but the Consent Judgment reports have been the public's only window into the actual probe readings. EGLE says it has no Rule 433(4) notices from GFL for 2020 to 2024.[6]

What is unknown: How is compliance determined? How long must the probes be less than LEL with the vents before EGLE will determine that compliance is achieved? How frequently will those measurements be taken? When and to whom will they be reported? Will it require paying FOIA fees to access the test results of the vents? Will anyone provide the results to first responders nearby?

The 2024 investigation, the 2025 well upgrades and the September 2026 vents all came with no penalties for the gas since the 2022 Consent Judgment. Is the work driven by the statewide methane migration push from 2023? By the March 7, 2027 date in the Consent Judgment? Or by GFL wanting to achieve compliance before the Washtenaw Board of Commissioners votes on its plans for a new landfill north of Six Mile Road? Why has it become so important now?

In the meantime, vents installed in Wayne County, with no record of notice to the township fire department, release untreated methane and whatever VOCs it carries into the air neighbors breathe.   No test of the vented gas is public; the only chemical lab test of probe gas, at 18D in 2024, found chloroform, which EPA screens as a cancer risk. Does anyone care?

If someone in Wayne County cares, who could act? The gas is regulated by EGLE, a state agency, and Wayne and Oakland County residents have state legislators. But the county's final approval on key decisions about Arbor Hills, including the materials management plan that will shape the new landfill north of Six Mile Road, rests with the Washtenaw County Board of Commissioners, which recently considered holding non-resident comments for the end of the meeting. The landfill's nearest residents in Wayne County (backyards bordering GFL's property) and Oakland County (less than 2 miles away) cannot vote for anyone on that board, yet its decisions arguably affect their lives more than those of most Washtenaw County residents.

No Michigan rule has been found that requires residents or neighbors to be told when methane above the Lower Explosive Limit is found beyond the licensed landfill's boundary. Should there be one?

EPA's Regional Screening Levels give a residential indoor-air level for each chemical: chloroform 0.12, benzene 0.36 and vinyl chloride 0.17 micrograms per cubic meter (each set at a one-in-a-million added cancer risk), and hydrogen sulfide 2.1 micrograms per cubic meter (a non-cancer level). EPA's vapor-intrusion guidance assumes soil gas is diluted about 33-fold as it seeps into a home (an attenuation factor of 0.03), so each indoor-air level is divided by 0.03 to get the matching soil-gas level: chloroform 4.0, benzene about 12, vinyl chloride about 5.7 and hydrogen sulfide about 70 micrograms per cubic meter. Lab results in parts per billion were converted to micrograms per cubic meter using each chemical's molecular weight at 25 °C. These are screening levels, not legal limits: a result above one means a closer look is warranted, not that anyone has been harmed. The soil-gas level is a yardstick for gas right under a home's foundation. Probe 18D is about 1,200 feet from the nearest homes, so the comparison shows how concentrated the gas is at the probe, not what is in anyone's basement. ↩︎
  5. 0.72 ppm of dimethyl disulfide is about 2,800 micrograms per cubic meter (molecular weight 94.2, at 25 °C). That is above Michigan's air-toxics screening level of 16 micrograms per cubic meter (annual average; 1,200 over 24 hours) and above the 0.5 ppm workplace limit (ACGIH, 8-hour shift), which is set for healthy adult workers, not round-the-clock exposure. As with chloroform, these compare gas at the probe, not air in anyone's home. ↩︎
