Hot Wells, an Outside Expert, and 852 Children

by Trisha Kunst · data scientist and resident living less than one mile from Arbor Hills · delivered November 18, 2026

The public comment Trisha Kunst gave in person to the Washtenaw County Materials Management Planning Committee on November 18, 2026, at the meeting where GFL presented on its environmental compliance at Arbor Hills with EGLE and YCUA staff in the room. She set aside her prepared remarks to respond to the presentation. Both versions are below, followed by the four handouts given to every committee member and the questions emailed to committee members that morning.

To: Washtenaw County Materials Management Planning Committee

From: Trisha Kunst, data scientist and resident less than 1 mile from Arbor Hills

Re: Hot gas wells at Arbor Hills, the outside expert offered to the committee, and the children at two nearby elementary schools

Delivered: November 18, 2026, Materials Management Planning Committee meeting (spoken, about two minutes)

The comment, as delivered

Hi everybody. I'm Trisha Kunst. I run arborhillsmonitor.org. I'm a local data scientist.

First I wanted to address some of the hot-well issues. They (EGLE) made it sound like it was back in 2021. It was then, but the clearest chemical signature of the oxidation happened in March of 2025. Not very long ago. [The methane-to-carbon-dioxide warning sign appeared in 2021 to 2022 and again in early 2025; see fact check item 8.]

Why does anybody care that trash is hot? The EPA told us in the early 2000s that for every 18 degrees (Fahrenheit) the temperature goes up, the NMOCs a landfill produces double. [Correction: this rule of thumb comes from ATSDR's 2001 Landfill Gas Primer, not EPA; see fact check item 10.] That's an exponential rate. So every time the temperature goes up, the neighbors are exposed to more NMOC emissions. That's the problem with hot wells.

All of you received the personal cell phone number of a senior engineer who worked on Chiquita Canyon. You were asked to call him and educate yourselves. He offered to brief the commissioners. As of today, nobody has called him.

I'd also like to point out that there are two elementary schools, not one, with 852 children. That means Arbor Hills wins the bronze: it ranks third in Michigan for the number of elementary children going to school less than a mile from a landfill. And one personal question… [The chair called time.]

Trisha Kunst
Data scientist, resident less than 1 mile from Arbor Hills
trisha@arborhillsmonitor.org

Lightly edited for clarity; corrections in brackets [ ].

  • 852 children is the Spring 2026 state headcount for Salem Elementary (401 students) and Ridge Wood Elementary (451 students), both less than one mile from the landfill. The number counts students enrolled at the two schools, not children living nearby. Of the 45 Michigan Type II landfills measured, two have more elementary students that close, which puts Arbor Hills third. That ranking counts every student at one Granger school, including grades 6 to 8; counting grades K to 5 only, Arbor Hills would rank second.

The prepared remarks

The script Trisha brought to the podium and set aside. It was not read aloud. It is included because it covers capacity, the passive vents, PFAS testing and the county's revenue, which the spoken comment did not.

I'm Trisha Kunst, a local data scientist. I run arborhillsmonitor.org.

There is no capacity problem. Even with no new landfill, Washtenaw's trash would not have to travel more than 50 miles for about 16 to 18 years. And the truck emissions saved by a shorter haul are small next to the landfill gas a new landfill would release for decades.

Meanwhile, the burden is already here. On September 28th and 29th, GFL's consultant installed two passive vents east of Napier Road. Since then, they have released soil gas straight into the open air. The last time that gas was tested for toxic compounds, in June 2024, it held chloroform about 70 times EPA's screening level for soil gas under homes. No test of what the vents release is on record.

In the groundwater, GFL itself proposed testing for PFAS, including PFOS, in July 2023, to start once EGLE approved. That testing has never started. Please use the leverage the county already has under its 2017 host agreement to get it started.

852 children attend Salem Elementary and Ridge Wood Elementary, both less than a mile from the waste.

So what is left? The money. Almost five million dollars a year: about 1.3 to 1.4 million for Washtenaw County, and about 3.5 million for Salem Township.

For how long must residents, and those 852 children, carry the burden of this revenue?

Notes on the prepared remarks

  • The July 2023 PFAS testing plan was submitted under an EGLE consent order (MMD Consent Order 115-05-2023).
  • "Never started" rests on the record: in response to a public-records request (FOIA E613160), EGLE reported no approval of the plan and no groundwater PFAS results after April 2023.
  • The truck emissions rest on an unpublished estimate that has not been through independent review.
  • Of Salem Township's roughly $3.5 million (fiscal year ending March 31, 2025, as reported by the Detroit Free Press, Jan. 7, 2026), about $2.6 million is landfill-gas revenue from the existing landfill, which would continue for some time after the landfill stopped taking waste. The county figure is from the committee's July 8 and September 9, 2026 meeting slides.

The questions emailed to committee members

Only committee members can ask questions at a Materials Management Planning Committee meeting. At 7:00 AM on November 18, Trisha emailed these questions to committee members, with copies to the county staff and planning consultant who support the committee (three copies bounced and were resent at 9:05 AM), asking that a member read one or more of them aloud while GFL, EGLE and YCUA were present. The email text is reproduced below; addresses are omitted.

Dear Committee Members,

Thank you for your work on the county's materials management plan. Residents near Arbor Hills have questions we hope can be answered at today's meeting, while GFL, EGLE and YCUA are in the room.

Only members can ask questions at the meeting, so I'm asking for your help. Would one of you be willing to ask one or more of the questions below on residents' behalf?

Each is written to be read aloud as is and is based on public records, most of them from EGLE. I'm glad to send the source for any of them. A member might open with: "Residents have shared a few questions with me, and I'd like to ask them while GFL, EGLE and YCUA are here."

I've also built a free tool for the committee's use on capacity: policy.arborhillsmonitor.org/capacity. It uses EGLE's FY2025 landfill report to show how much landfill space Southeast Michigan has left. You can choose which landfills count, change the recycling rate, food waste and Canadian waste assumptions, and save the results and assumptions to a PDF.

Questions

  1. For GFL: In June 2021 GFL proposed a fix for methane migrating through the soil under Napier Road. More than five years later, quarterly reports still show methane above the lower explosive limit, at its peak more than 18 times that limit, and a 2024 lab report found the landfill is likely at least a partial source. What are you doing next, and when will the boundary probes read under the limit?
  2. For GFL: The two passive vents installed east of Napier Road in late September release soil gas straight into open air, untreated. The only lab test of either probe's gas for toxic compounds, in June 2024, found chloroform at about 70 times EPA's soil-gas screening level for homes. That was gas in the ground, not air in a home. Are these vents part of the landfill's gas collection system, and why release this gas into the air instead of sending it to a flare? Have you tested what the vents are releasing now, and will you share those results?
  3. For GFL: On September 28 and 29, two passive vents were installed beside gas probes east of Napier Road to release methane from the soil. A nearby probe, about 1,050 feet from the nearest homes, passed the lower explosive limit for the first time on August 27 at 7.6 percent methane, fell to zero on September 16 and 25, then read 24.9 percent on September 29, nearly five times the limit. Was that spike related to installing the vents the day before and the day of that reading? Are the vents lowering the methane, and how will residents know before your next quarterly report? What safety advice would you give residents in the meantime?
  4. For EGLE: Salem Elementary, about 3,200 feet from the landfill, has no municipal water available and gets its drinking water from a well. EGLE's public PFAS results for that well show six rounds of testing from December 2020 to February 2025, all non-detect. Is more testing of that well planned, and would EGLE consider testing it for compounds such as 1,4-dioxane and vinyl chloride?
  5. For GFL: In April 2023, a groundwater well at the southeast corner of the landfill had PFOS, one of the PFAS "forever chemicals," at nearly four times the state's drinking-water criterion, and PFAS in that well had been over the criteria in every sample since 2019. In July 2023, under an EGLE consent order, your consultant submitted a plan to EGLE to test four wells for PFAS twice a year. No PFAS groundwater results have been released since April 2023. Has that testing started, and if not, when will it?
  6. For GFL: Landfill gas carries more than methane, including toxic chemicals. A September 2026 test of your collected gas, after the treatment system where it enters the flares, found benzene at about 2.5 to 3 ppm and vinyl chloride, a known human carcinogen, at about 0.3 ppm. How do you track the toxic chemicals your landfill releases, both from the surface and through the gas you collect and burn or process?
  7. For GFL: EGLE's June 2026 inspection found about half of the landfill's vertical gas wells showing signs of impairment, and about 200 pumps have been installed to remove liquid from the wells. What have you changed in the proposed new landfill's design so its gas wells don't end up the same way?
  8. For GFL: Your September 9 drawings for the proposed new landfill show gas wells but not boundary probes. Across Napier Road from the new site are a township park with community gardens, the historic Thayer schoolhouse, and homes. Probes east of Napier at the current site have read above the explosive limit for years. Where will you place probes at the new site, will they sit between the waste and those homes, and will any of the property for the new landfill fall within the county plan's 300-foot park setback?
  9. For GFL: The wetland application for the proposed new landfill says it would permanently fill about 33 acres of regulated wetlands, and those wetlands continue onto adjacent land. EGLE has described the stream on that parcel as "a headwater stream of Johnson Drain and the Rouge River," and on EGLE's pre-application form for inland lakes and streams, you answered "Yes" to "Lakes or Streams involved?" Johnson Drain, also called Johnson Creek, is described as a groundwater-fed, cold-water trout stream. Your September 9 drawings show the new landfill's base dug well below today's ground. How will you keep it separated from that groundwater, a slurry wall like the current site's or something else, and how will you protect both the creek and nearby residents' well water?
  10. For GFL: In your August 26, 2025 letter to YCUA, you wrote that your leachate "pretreatment system does not consistently treat leachate below the new Maximum Limits for PFOA and PFOS," and that you had already pilot-tested two PFAS treatment methods. In May 2026, you told YCUA a permanent pretreatment system was expected by February 2027. Which method did you choose, is that system on schedule, and how will it handle the added leachate from the new site?

If there's time

  1. For GFL: Your perimeter air monitors measure hydrogen sulfide. On September 14, monitor MS-1 read 153 parts per billion, more than twice Michigan's 72 ppb health-screening level and five times California's 30 ppb limit. In June 2025, MS-3 read 327 ppb. What caused the September 14 reading, did you notify EGLE or residents, and what did you change afterward? What advice would you give residents with asthma? Should they stay indoors when hydrogen sulfide is high at the perimeter?
  2. For GFL: Probes 19 through 22 sit on your east property line, the probes nearest the homes to the east. Your consultant's October 30 report lists 'Monthly gas monitoring at probes 17S, 18S and 19-22,' but its data tables have no readings at all for probe 21 and only one, from May, for probe 22. Were probes 21 and 22 read in July, August and September, and if so, where are the readings?
  3. For GFL: Under the law, rain that falls on open waste in the new cells is considered leachate and must be treated. Your September 9 drawings show that leachate would travel by double-walled pipe from the new site, across Six Mile Road, to your treatment facility. The landfill has had leachate releases before, including one in 2022 that reached a tributary of Johnson Creek. How will that pipe be watched for leaks, and what happens if it fails?
  4. For GFL: In 2022, EGLE found that leachate mixed with stormwater had entered the landfill's detention pond and drained to a tributary of Johnson Creek. Your September 9 drawings show two retention basins at the new site, with outlet channels running to the headwater stream S1, another tributary of Johnson Creek. How do you plan to intercept the stormwater from the active area and redirect it to the leachate treatment system, rather than to those basins?

No reply is needed. I've sent this with everyone's address hidden so it doesn't become a discussion among members outside the meeting.

Thank you for considering it. See you at the meeting,
Trisha Kunst
